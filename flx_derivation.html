Differentiating a Program

FLUXION returns the derivatives of a C++ computation: exact to the representation, up to order four, mixed partial derivatives included, on scalar values as on SIMD registers.

Programs as compositions

Evaluating a program on an input performs a finite sequence of elementary operations, each of them an addition, a multiplication, a comparison, or a call to an elementary function. Write \(v_0 = a\) for the input, \(N\) for the number of operations the evaluation performs, and \(\varphi_i\) for the one performed at step \(i\). Each step reads the values already computed, and the last one is the result:

\[ v_i = \varphi_i(v_0, \dots, v_{i-1}) \quad (1 \le i \le N), \qquad f(a) = v_N. \]

A program is therefore a composition, and differentiating what it computes requires two things: the derivative of each elementary operation, and a rule giving the derivative of a composition from the derivatives of its parts. Nothing else enters.

Derivative at a point

For \(f\) defined around \(a\), the derivative at \(a\) is the limit of the difference quotient,

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}, \]

when that limit exists. The same statement written as an expansion is the form used from here on:

\[ f(a+h) = f(a) + f'(a)\,h + o(h). \]

Near \(a\), \(f\) agrees with an affine function, and \(f'(a)\) is the coefficient of its linear part. A derivative is a number attached to a point, and a formula for \(f'\) is one way of obtaining that number.

For an elementary operation that formula is known in closed form. We know, for instance, that

\[ (x^n)' = n\,x^{n-1}, \qquad (\sin x)' = \cos x, \qquad (e^x)' = e^x, \qquad (\log x)' = \frac{1}{x} \ \ (x > 0), \]

and each of these is evaluated as cheaply as the function it comes from. Every \(\varphi_i\) of a program has such a formula, which settles the first of the two requirements.

Rules of computation

Four rules give the derivative of a compound expression from the derivatives of its parts:

\[ (u+v)' = u' + v', \qquad (uv)' = u'v + uv', \qquad \left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}, \qquad (f \circ g)'(a) = f'\big(g(a)\big)\,g'(a). \]

The last of them settles the second requirement. It gives \((f \circ g)'(a)\) from \(g'(a)\) and from \(f'\) taken at \(g(a)\), two values of functions already known in closed form, at points that evaluating \(f\big(g(a)\big)\) passes through anyway. No expression for \((f \circ g)'\) is formed.

Carrying a derivative

Applied to the sequence of Programs as compositions once per operation, the chain rule produces a second sequence beside it,

\[ d_0 = 1, \qquad d_i = \sum_{j<i} \frac{\partial \varphi_i}{\partial v_j}(v_0,\dots,v_{i-1})\, d_j, \]

with \(d_N = f'(a)\). Every partial derivative appearing there is that of a single elementary operation, taken from the closed forms above, and the sum has as many terms as that operation has arguments, so two at most for the arithmetic operations.

The two sequences are computed together, \(d_i\) needing the operands of \(v_i\) and the \(d_j\) already obtained. Each value therefore carries its own derivative, \(f'(a)\) is available when the program returns, and no expression for \(f'\) is written anywhere.

What is differentiated is the sequence this input went through. A branch on a comparison, a loop whose count depends on the data, a value read from a table: the execution selects one path, and what comes back is the derivative of that path. abs at zero returns \(-1\) or \(1\) according to the comparison it took, where the function itself has no derivative.

Higher orders, and several arguments

Differentiating \(f'\) gives \(f''\), and the operation repeats as long as what it returns stays differentiable. For a function of \(m\) arguments, the partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x_j\) is the derivative of the function of \(x_j\) alone obtained by holding the other arguments fixed. Two objects are built from these:

  • the gradient, the \(m\) first partial derivatives at the point;
  • the Hessian, the \(m^2\) second ones, of which \(m(m+1)/2\) are distinct when the second partials are continuous, since \(\partial^2 f/\partial x\,\partial y\) and \(\partial^2 f/\partial y\,\partial x\) agree there.

A program takes several inputs, and one derivative rarely answers what is asked of it. Optimisation and the solution of nonlinear systems want the gradient at a point, a second-order method wants the Hessian as well, and an iteration wants them again at every step. Each of these is a set of numbers to be carried through the same evaluation, and how many there are fixes the size of the value the arithmetic works on.

FLUXION holds a value and the derivatives asked of it in one number, so the second sequence is computed by the arithmetic itself. Obtaining a Derivative weighs that choice against the other ways of obtaining the same result.
